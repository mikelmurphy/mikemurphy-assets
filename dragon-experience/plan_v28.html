1. What is Vilagozd?

Vilagozd combines the Slovenian words for fairy and forest. It is our own world of stories based on the landscapes, customs and legends of Slovenia. It includes dragons, fairies, unicorns, elves, magical bees and people whose lives cross the border between the ordinary and the fantastic.

Vilagozd is first an intellectual-property (IP) company. That means its most important assets are its original stories, characters, art and ideas. These can live in books, films, games, toys and real places. The first physical attraction is meant to introduce that world to paying guests in a practical way. We want proof of what people enjoy before making a much larger investment in a family resort.

Why Ljubljana has a natural starting point

Ljubljana is already known as the City of Dragons. The dragon is on its coat of arms and its famous Dragon Bridge. Visitors often arrive knowing that symbol. We see a simple unmet wish: people can take a photo of a dragon statue, but they cannot yet step into a full, high-quality dragon fantasy world built around Slovenian stories. That is the need we want to test. [1]

Our aim is an experience adults will choose for a date or a city break, while families and schools also have something rich to discover. It is not a children’s play centre with a dragon painted on the wall. It is an original fantasy place with the attention to detail people expect from major themed attractions.

The story guests step into

Imagine an eccentric old journalist and collector who is sure a secret place called Vilagozd exists in Slovenia. He believes the forest is home to fairies, dragons, unicorns and more. Over many years, he has gathered maps, notes, old stories and what he calls bones, fossils and relics of creatures that science has never found.

He has also paid local artists to show what the hidden world might look like. Guests enter his odd private collection, then walk into the artists’ interpretations of the six magical realms. The story makes the experience feel connected. It also lets local makers create new art, sounds, puzzles and objects without pretending our fantasy is real history.

THE RULE THAT PROTECTS OUR STORY
The collector and his supposed finds are fictional. Displays will clearly separate true Slovenian culture, folk stories and invented Vilagozd fantasy. Cultural advisers and Slovenian artists will help us get real traditions right.

2. Why this attraction comes first

Use a smaller physical business to test the power of our stories before we commit to a major resort.

We looked at several ways to launch Vilagozd. A temporary pop-up is less expensive, but may be too small to prove guests will pay for a full fantasy world. A hotel, indoor waterpark or resort could be a strong business later, but needs much more land, construction, staff and time. Because Vilagozd creates stories, the best first test is a place where the stories themselves sell the ticket.

An existing building can help us. We do not need to buy resort land just to open our first attraction. A well-placed venue also gives us people who already come into Ljubljana or BTC City. The savings are not automatic: we still need a long lease, building work, safety approval and a real cost plan. But the first risk can be smaller and easier to measure than a new destination resort.

Why Meow Wolf is the main model

Meow Wolf opened House of Eternal Return in Santa Fe, New Mexico, in March 2016. Santa Fe is a small city compared with the major US markets where Meow Wolf later expanded. Its first site was inside a former bowling alley, not a purpose-built resort. Guests searched for clues and walked into strange rooms made by artists. This is close to the kind of creative business we want to build. [6–9]

The co-founder said they thought about 150,000 annual visits were possible. Meow Wolf later reported around 400,000 visits and $6.8 million in revenue in its first twelve months, followed by nearly 500,000 visits during 2017. Its original exhibit cost about $3 million. George R. R. Martin separately spent about $3 million to buy and repair the building, then leased it to the operator. It would be wrong to call the entire project a $3 million build. [6–9]

Santa Fe also draws tourists and people from the wider New Mexico region, including Albuquerque. It is not a perfect match for Ljubljana. Its ticket prices, taxes, building arrangements and operating costs were different. The lesson is not that Ljubljana will sell the same number of tickets. The lesson is that an original story in a reused building can become a destination that people will pay to explore.

A premium product, not a cheap imitation

Our place must look and feel special. We want layered art, convincing environments, strong lighting and sound, hidden stories, and details that reward a second look. We will aim for the polish of a major themed attraction while keeping the building and effects within the approved investment limit. This is a quality goal, not proof that we can afford every effect we imagine.

London now has other premium immersive products, though they are not exact copies of Meow Wolf. FRAMELESS is a projection-led art experience that recommends about two hours and advertises adult online tickets starting around £29.50. Phantom Peak is an interactive, free-roaming story town; its new Stratford location was advertised for opening in December 2026. These show other ways that operators package immersive entertainment, not proof that our €27 ticket assumption will work in Ljubljana. [13–14]

OUR FIRST STRATEGIC CHOICE
Choose one quality, stand-alone Ljubljana attraction. Use its paid visitor data, guest responses and repeat sales to decide whether to open more city attractions and what should go into the future resort. Neither expansion path is needed for the first attraction to pay its own bills.

3. What guests will experience

Guests do not watch one show in a fixed order. They explore rooms, touch safe objects, follow clues, take photographs and discover more if they wish. A normal visit should take roughly 90–120 minutes, with easy choices for children and deeper stories for adults. We want visitors to leave feeling that they found the world of Vilagozd for themselves.

Six areas at opening: the collection and five magical worlds

1. The collector’s private collection An odd study filled with maps, journals, purported dragon bones and fairy relics. Visitors are told the collector is fictional. Real stories of Slovenia are presented accurately beside the artist-made fantasy.

2. Enchanted Forest A rich forest of fairies and hidden details, with safe climbing or sliding only where the site allows. Guests can join a playful oath to protect the land. Adults can enjoy the art without needing to play.

3. The Cave System One linked cave with dragons and the folk-inspired škratje. A whistle may wake sounds and lights. A large dragon puppet and small dragons offer the main dragon wish-fulfilment moment.

4. Unicorn Maze A maze of clues and illusions linked to Princess Marjeta, King Matjaž and the Unicorn Sword. Guests can choose an easy walk or spend longer solving puzzles.

5. Elven Quest Music, light, tunnels and small mysteries introduce Eirlys, a character from the Vilagozd stories. There must also be a clear step-free route.

6. The Beehive A warm, gentle area for younger children, around ages two to six, where magical colorful bees live. Adults learn about real Slovenian beekeeping while children play. The optional creative kit is described below.

How Slovenian culture becomes part of the fun

Slovenia’s beekeeping is on UNESCO’s list of living cultural heritage. Local beekeepers, traditional painted bee panels and the Carniolan honeybee give us real stories to teach. The fictional magical bees are our own creation. These must never be mixed up. We also want accurate explanations of dragons in Ljubljana lore, fairies in traditional stories and the Kurenti rounds linked to the carnival season. [1,15–16]

Additions after the first trading year

In Year 2 we plan to add the Pust Carnival realm and an optional interactive upcharge device, later called the Iskra (Slovenian for “spark”). Pust would introduce masks, rhythm, costumes and the fictional Kurent character Zoran. Iskra would make some lights, sounds and characters respond to a guest’s actions. Neither addition is needed to enjoy the basic ticket.

In Year 3 we may add a compact dragon thrill element. It could draw on dry-slide technology or another safe active experience. An indoor zip coaster is only a concept, not a committed ride. Any moving device needs engineering, insurance, safety certification, space and a supplier quotation before approval.

Why one part is designed for very young children

The attraction should work for all ages. Still, parents with young children need an area that feels safe and calm. The Beehive lets us test our stories and products for preschoolers without turning the whole business into a soft-play centre. We can learn which bee characters children remember, which activities parents value and whether anyone buys a take-home kit.

WHAT THE CUSTOMER BUYS
A normal ticket includes every open realm and the full main story. Guests can choose extra purchases, but no one must buy a bracelet or a kit to enjoy the visit.

A detailed look at the first Meow Wolf business

Meow Wolf opened House of Eternal Return in Santa Fe in March 2016. Its first venue was about 1,858m² of exhibit inside a larger former bowling-alley property. Reported operator-funded scenic cost was about $2.5–2.7m; separately, the landlord financed around $2.7–3m of property and renovation. These are different payers. It is misleading to call the whole project a $3m building.

The company promoted roughly 400,000 guests and $6.8m revenue during its first 12 months. For calendar 2016 (a different, shorter period), reviewed company statements show about $5.52m revenue. In 2017 its publicly filed consolidated statement reported $9.227m sales and near 500,000 visits at the Santa Fe location. The full company included more than just admissions and should not be presented as a site-only net margin.

4. Why we believe people may come

There are two groups we want to serve: people already visiting Ljubljana, and people who live nearby and want something new to do. The city’s dragon symbol gives us a clear reason for tourists to be interested. Families and adults in the region give us a reason to create fresh content and sell repeat visits.

We believe the market may support 200,000–300,000 paid visits each year. This is a goal to test, not a visitor forecast proven by research. Other successful attractions show that people in Slovenia do pay for culture, discovery and family experiences. They do not tell us how many will buy our tickets at our planned price.

Why we believe the city is missing this kind of product

Ljubljana has fun attractions, science activities, museums, play centres and shops. But we have not found a large, permanent, premium walk-through dragon world that combines original fantasy, Slovenian culture, interactive art and optional quests. This is our proposed gap in the market. It must still be tested through real customer research and competitive mapping.

How we test demand before we build

We will ask locals, hotel guests, school leaders, tourists, couples and corporate event buyers which part they would pay to see. We will show them real design samples and several ticket prices. We will measure paid deposits or honest booking tests—not just people saying they like dragons.

5. Where should it open?

We have not selected a building. Our first test is about 2,000 m² of usable space, which must include room for guests, safe walkways, staff, toilets, sales, services and the art itself. A three-storey city building might work, but stairs, lifts and escape routes can cut down useful area. The final area depends on a design and safety study.

Three choices that serve different customers

Location choice Why it could win What must be proved
Ljubljana city centre Tourists already walk to the attractions and landmarks. Strong walking traffic is an advantage for last-minute admission. A building large enough, with affordable total rent, good access, valid permits and safe movement between floors.
Railway Museum / nearby industrial buildings An old workshop could make a remarkable setting. The railway institution may value a long-term tenant and help restore unused space. Who controls the exact building, what can be repaired, who pays, what public approvals apply, walking routes and long lease rights.
BTC City Ljubljana Very large shopping and leisure district, car access and established activity operators such as WOOP!, Minicity and Atlantis. Exact entertainment-zone footfall, parking, rent, visitor conversion and whether tourists will travel there.

The founder has seen a former industrial building at the railway site that may suit the project. We want to explore a lease or partnership under which the property owner funds some of the building repairs because the attraction gives the site a long-term purpose. This is a proposal, not an agreed public grant or an offer from the railway authority. The estimated walk from the centre must be checked from the actual building entrance. [21]

If most buyers prove to be international tourists, the city centre or a good railway-area site may be strongest. If locals and repeat family visitors are the larger market, BTC’s parking and existing leisure activity may matter more. We should let the research and full property cost choose the location rather than decide in advance.

How we keep the building from hurting the business

Compare the whole cost: rent, service charges, heat, power, repairs, property taxes if any, access, and what it takes to prepare the site. We want a lease long enough to earn back the fit-out, rights to renew, permission to use the story and installations, and a legal path to sell or move the business. A lower rent is valuable only if the repairs, access and legal risks do not cost more.

SITE RULE
No lease is signed on the strength of foot traffic alone. We need a safe design, clear landlord work, realistic rent and an independent visitor-flow study before using the building in the investor case.

6. How the experience earns money

The main business is admission. But a visitor may choose to spend more on a gift, personalized quest, food, a party or a group activity. Good additional products can improve average guest spend. They must add to the experience, not turn it into a series of payment requests.

The Beehive: a product children make themselves

The Beehive can go beyond a play room. Children learn that bees help make food, honey and wax. With help, they pick the colors for a crayon set, make a personalized label or box, print their own coloring pages and choose a fluffy magical bee to take home. Parents decide whether to pay for the optional set.

This is our own proposed product. It draws on the personal-making idea seen at Crayola Experience and Build-A-Bear. It is not a licence from either company. The key test is whether the margin, time per child and cleaning work make sense on a busy day. Slovenian beekeeping is genuine UNESCO-listed heritage; our magical bees are fiction. [15,22]

The Iskra: an extra adventure you can take home

Great Wolf Lodge sells MagiQuest wands and game time. The same wand can be used again. Universal sells interactive wands for Harry Potter areas and Power-Up Bands for Super Nintendo World. These are separate examples of paid devices that add games or personal progress to an already themed place. We want to test a simpler Vilagozd device called Iskra. [10–12]

Iskra could make parts of the forest light up, start a character response or record a finished quest. It must be optional and work for people who do not use a phone. We will test breakage, battery life, cleaning, staff time, privacy and manufacturing cost before Year 2 approval.

The Iskra: an optional interactive upcharge device

Iskra means “spark” in Slovenian. A guest could buy a seven-color crystal that unlocks responses in the experience and carries progress across visits. Each color would stand for a positive trait, such as courage, kindness, imagination or curiosity. Guests would choose freely; it would not be a psychological diagnosis or a claim about a child’s personality.

The crystal could clip into a wand, bracelet, necklace, ring or other accessory. These are possible upgrades, not funded product lines today. The base admission must remain complete without a device. We would test initial device purchase, accessory purchases, repeat visits, hardware reliability and rights to use customer data before approving full production.

In the approved financial model, the device begins in the second trading year, with an assumed €30 gross selling price, 20% of paid visits purchasing, and €10 assumed landed cost. These are modeling assumptions, not measured Universal or Great Wolf Lodge conversion rates. Neither operator publicly supplies a comparable audited wand attach rate or per-site wand revenue figure in the sources reviewed.

Comparable product mechanics: Universal’s interactive Harry Potter wands and Super Nintendo World bands, and Great Wolf Lodge’s MagiQuest wands and costume-style bundles. They show product possibilities, not confirmed Slovenian demand or a license to copy protected designs.

Fresh reasons to come back

We would reuse the same rooms but change selected lighting, quests, characters and small props across the year. Winter: Pust, a Slovenian late-winter carnival in which masked traditions symbolically drive winter away and welcome spring. Spring: bees, flowers and new quests. Summer: family tourist challenges. Autumn: a spooky but age-rated dragon story. December: a winter-lights celebration. This tests repeat visits without rebuilding major sets every season.

The Pust themed realm is scheduled for the second trading year, with a February promotional season once the room is ready. A June launch date may require adjusting which February counts as that second trading-year campaign. The cultural team will distinguish real Kurenti traditions from Vilagozd’s fictional Zoran character.

Food, adults, groups and events

Adult guests may pay for a premium night with themed drinks and a private tour. Companies could book a team quest followed by food and drinks. Schools need clear lesson aims, teacher rates and suitable time slots. Birthday parties could be held in a dedicated room. Any alcohol service, catering or private event use requires the right permits, capacity and qualified operator.

Two questions we track each week

How many people paid to enter, and how much did they spend in total? We will report admission money, shop money, optional devices, kits, food and events separately. We also track what each sale costs to serve. This prevents large sales figures from hiding weak margins.

How food and events increase spending

A small high-quality drinks offer can add value, especially for adults and private evening use. Test signature non-alcoholic drinks first. Any alcoholic cocktail programme needs a licensed operator, appropriate staff, separate 18+ service controls, and product-specific tax treatment. The preferred starting model is a concession partner paying rent or a share of verified sales, rather than building a kitchen inside the original €5m cap.

Track revenue per visitor by channel, average total party bill, food/retail attachment, gross margin after product costs, capacity used by events, and net contribution after extra staffing. Selling more items is not the same as earning more cash.

7. How we attract guests and learn from them

The attraction should be easy to understand from one short video: Ljubljana is the City of Dragons, and now there is a world guests can step into. The video should show real art and real rooms, not promise effects that are not built.

Four main visitor groups

Tourists: A premium new activity near the city’s landmarks, with easy booking and clear Slovenian and English information. Test German, Italian and other markets after demand data.

Local families: Something worth choosing for a weekend, rainy day, school holiday or birthday. Bring them back when new rooms and quests appear.

Adults and teenagers: Mystery, visual art, hidden stories, high-quality sound and light, and evening experiences. The whole place must work without a child.

Schools and businesses: A true cultural or learning aim alongside a fun quest, simple group rates, useful lesson material and reliable arrival times.

A simple way to grow sales

A person finds a photo, video, hotel recommendation or review. They visit the website, understand the experience and see prices. They book a time, scan a ticket and enjoy the visit. Afterwards they may choose to join an email list, enter a properly run contest, refer a friend or book a return visit.

We want a large, useful permission-based marketing list, not a pile of names we cannot use. Offer adults genuine value: new story launches, prizes with clear rules, seasonal invitations and referral rewards. Ask for permission, explain what data we keep, make it easy to leave and never collect children’s personal information without the right parental safeguards. Every giveaway must follow Slovenian and EU marketing and prize rules.

The attraction is also a test lab for our stories

Ask guests which characters they remember, which rooms they enjoyed, what they would buy and whether they would come back. Look at group results, not at the private identity of a child. We will compare adults, teenagers, tourists, locals and families. If the dragon cave is most loved, that tells us something different from the Beehive being most loved. Both results could shape future media and resort decisions.

8. How we run it safely and well

A great room is useless if people cannot get through it safely or if the dragon breaks every weekend. A premium ticket needs good service, clean spaces, working effects and clear arrival times. We will hire an experienced manager and make a full operating plan before opening.

The model uses 320 open days per year and a visit of about 90 minutes. At 210,000 visits, that is about 656 guests per open day on average. But the busiest hours can be far above the average. Every room, passage, play area and exit needs a crowd test. The current draft capacity screen does not prove that a 300,000-visit year can fit.

Peak operations may need about 12 people on duty at once. This is not total staffing. One scenario at 200,000 paid visits uses roughly 22.19 yearly full-time-equivalent staff. We need a real rota that includes days off, vacations, sick leave, training, cleaning, security, a technician, first aid and possible Year 3 ride staff.

The staff and safety plan

  • General manager accountable for sales, staffing, quality and cash.

  • Front desk, booking and timed entry staff, with special handling of groups.

  • Room guides trained in guest care, effects, accessible routes and child safety.

  • Extra monitoring for younger children and any active slides or future thrill feature.

  • Cleaning, repairs, fire safety, security, first aid and a response plan if technology fails.

  • Clear supplier maintenance contracts and ownership of lighting, media and control software.

How we keep a premium standard

Before opening, test with first-time visitors. Find where they get lost, bored or uneasy. Staff must help without ruining the mystery. Each major effect needs a safe backup. We will maintain what works and refresh scenes when the business can afford it.

A great experience must also be a great place to work

The people who welcome guests, look after children, tell stories and fix technology will define Vilagozd’s reputation. Building an admired place to work is a core operating goal, not an optional HR programme. We want people to stay, grow, tell friends to join, and feel trusted to make a guest’s day better.

10. Seven goals that make the plan real

A strong story and a good business are different things. We will do the small, testable work first. The big construction contract comes only after the evidence is good enough.

2. Choose the site A safe venue of suitable size with a long, usable lease. Rent, building works or rights cost too much.
3. Design the world Premium quality and safe six-area opening fit the cost limit. Important scenes cannot be delivered reliably.

GOAL 1 OF 7

Prove that enough people will buy a ticket

A good idea is not a good business until people pay for it.

WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE
A demand study and real booking tests support at least 210,709 paid first-year visits at €27 (the 15% threshold), ideally the 215,000 at €28 underwriting case, or show a different affordable design.

Who leads it: Independent demand research firm, attraction marketing lead and finance lead (all proposed).

Before we move on: No full build contract if an outside study cannot support the paid visits and ticket price needed by the investor case.

Understand who wants to visit

We need more than a total tourism number. We need to know which people will buy and on which days.

  • Interview and test real parents, tourists, adults and teachers. Record where they live, what they would pay and when they would come.

  • Separate local repeat trips from holiday visits. Do not count one tourist or one BTC shopper as a guaranteed customer.

  • Use a small physical preview and clear price choices. Track actual refundable deposits or early bookings when legal.

Proof needed: An independent report with expected paid visits, price, season, buyer groups and uncertainty ranges.

Show the price works

The model needs a blended gross ticket value around €27. That is not the same as putting a €27 sticker on every ticket.

  • Test adult, child, family, group and discount ticket bundles.

  • Include commissions, refunds and the right VAT when working out money kept from a ticket.

  • Compare demand at each price instead of assuming every visitor buys the top offer.

Proof needed: A tested price mix that gives the attraction enough net revenue to work.

Check the number fits the building

An attraction cannot sell more tickets than its rooms and staff can safely handle.

  • Map arrivals by day and hour, then test the busiest holiday afternoon.

  • Time how long groups stay in each world; check queues and emergency exits.

  • Rerun the full finance case at the final tested volume.

Proof needed: The researcher, safety engineer and finance lead agree on the same visitor figure.

Find a building that protects the business

A successful location could be a long-term city business and a way to introduce more guests to Vilagozd. It can add new stories, events and optional quests while serving tourists and residents. This is the simplest path.

Open a new location or license the format

Meow Wolf went from Santa Fe to other cities. The Museum of Illusions group has also copied a city-attraction model across many countries. Our own first site could help us build an operating guide, supplier list, approved art rights, guest-service standard and measured unit economics. A local partner might then build a second Vilagozd experience. No such partner is signed. [3,6–9]

A portable touring version is a different business. Large sculptures, effects and built rooms are not automatically easy to move. Before calling this a pop-up model, we would price transport, packing, repeated assembly, repairs, storage, insurance and venue downtime. A smaller touring show might make more sense than moving an entire permanent installation.

Build awareness for the future resort

Longer term, we would like sites within the intended resort’s four-hour driving market. If a family enjoys Vilagozd in a city near home, that could give them a reason to visit a larger resort in Slovenia later. It also helps us test different countries and languages before making resort choices. The future resort has no right or duty to buy the attraction and no resort income is counted here.

WHY THIS MATTERS TO THE PARENT COMPANY
The attraction is both a possible stand-alone earnings business and a learning tool for an original story company. Its main value is shown by actual paid guests and cash, not by an unverified value assigned to future movies or resorts.

The first 90 days

We should spend first on clear evidence, not a large scenic build. The roles below have to be appointed or confirmed.

Period Actions Decision evidence
Days 1–30 Appoint independent visitor researchers. Request plans and written terms for city, railway and BTC options. Ask an attraction designer and construction cost expert for the first scope test. Two or three real sites, source-backed demand brief, room and safety constraints, spending control.
Days 31–60 Show paid sample scenes to different age groups; test premium price choices and group programs. Map queues and how a 2,000 m² venue might work. Price the large cave, puppet, Beehive kit and technical systems. Independent ticket study, busy-day flow model, manufacturing ranges and site comparison.
Days 61–90 Choose the preferred site or reject all three. Get full landlord duties, legal lease review and staged vendor bids. Rerun investor cash from the final realistic attendance and costs. Written decision to proceed, redesign or stop. No automatic construction approval.

The risks we watch

Risk Warning sign Response
Premium ticket does not sell Tests show a smaller market at the planned gross price Change product or price, cut spend or stop
Wrong building Too little usable space, high repairs, weak rights or difficult tourist access Pick another site
Demand peaks exceed capacity Unsafe crowding in cave or child area, long queues Lower timed admissions, redesign or stop
Optional products disappoint Bee kit or Iskra margin and uptake below test limits Change the add-on or do not launch it
Unquoted scenic build Supplier prices push total spend above the ceiling Value-engineer without losing quality, or stop
No buyer or no transferable lease Sale route cannot be supported Underwrite operations without sale; no promised exit

At every step the options are proceed, revise or stop. A good early refusal is cheaper than a weak opening.

Simple meanings of financial words

Paid visit: One used ticket; not a person walking past the entrance.

Gross ticket value: What a guest pays before VAT and other costs are removed.

EBITDA: A measure of operating profit before interest, tax and some accounting costs; not money available to pay investors.

15% annual return goal: A modeled target, not a promised interest rate.

Six times EBITDA: An example future business sale value; no actual buyer has agreed to it.

Protected cash reserve: Money held to help keep the business operating, not a fund for unexpected building extras.
